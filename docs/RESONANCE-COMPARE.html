Winning modes

Definition applied: every settled video becomes the same 8-axis template (each axis its within-channel percentile). The templates of settled videos at or over 3x their channel median are clustered (k-means, k = 4); the four centroids are the winning modes. A mode’s lift is the share over 3x among all settled videos nearest to it. mR = 100 · max over modes of [ cosine(video template, mode) · mode lift ÷ best lift ].

mR = 100 · max over modes k of [ cosine(template, mode_k) · lift_k ÷ max lift ] template = the video’s 8 within-channel percentiles, one polygon per video mode_k = centroid k of the settled ≥3x population’s templates (k-means, k = 4, pre-registered) lift_k = share over 3x among all settled videos nearest mode_k (base = 17.2%)

For the paper: Each video is represented as an eight-dimensional template, each component its within-channel percentile on one measured axis (outlier multiple, engagement rate, accumulation rate, likes, comments, length deviation, classifier forecast, title length). The templates of settled videos at or above 3x their channel median are partitioned by k-means (k = 4); the centroids define four recurring configurations, the modes. A mode’s lift is the proportion of all settled videos nearest that centroid that reached 3x. The score is mR = 100 · maxₖ [cos(t, cₖ) · Lₖ ÷ max L]: cosine similarity to the best-matching mode, weighted by that mode’s lift relative to the strongest mode. mR therefore measures proximity to a previously observed winning configuration, scaled by that configuration’s historical yield. It is not a probability, and it inherits the selection properties of the clustering: k is a pre-registered choice, and the modes are refit, and versioned, with the corpus.

For a 5th grader: We measure every video the same eight ways — like height, weight, and speed, but for videos. For each measurement we ask: compared with the other videos on its own channel, is this low or high? That turns every video into a shape, like a connect-the-dots drawing. We looked at all the videos that did way better than normal, and their drawings mostly match one of four shapes. Some of those shapes win a lot; one barely wins at all. To score a new video, we hold its drawing up against the four shapes and find the closest match. Match a winning shape closely and the score is high. Match nothing that has ever won, and the score is low — even if the video is huge.
